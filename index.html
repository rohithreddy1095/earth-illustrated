The shrinkage box

How to run it
  1. Grease a wooden or metal box, open on top, 30–40 mm square and 500–600 mm long.
  2. Wet the soil to a little past its liquid limit: a V-groove cut in it closes after about five taps on the box.
  3. Pack it in hard, corners first, and scrape the top level.
  4. Dry it seven days in the shade, or a day indoors and then in an oven at 110 °C.
  5. Push the bar to one end and measure the gap. Shrinkage is the gap over the wet length: FAO's example, 60 mm in a 600 mm box, is 10 %.

More than three large cracks besides the end gaps, and the soil is not suitable; more than 10 % shrinkage, and it is unfit for stabilising (FAO, Farm Structures).

Clay up close

Sizes from FAO: sand 2–0.06 mm, silt 0.06–0.002 mm, clay below 0.002 mm, so a sand grain can be a thousand times wider than a clay particle. Smectite clays such as montmorillonite can swell by up to 30 % in volume (Purdue Extension): water molecules push the mineral layers apart (NPTEL lecture notes), while kaolinite shows no shrink–swell to speak of (Burras, Iowa State). Black cotton soil is mostly montmorillonite (Katti 1978). The plates are drawn schematically.

Timber and bamboo

The wood in an earth house: why bamboo gets eaten, how to treat it, how culms are joined, and how strong it really is.

Inside a culm

Why bamboo gets eaten, and sources

Bamboo has no heartwood and almost none of the natural toxins that protect teak. Its inner tissue is packed with starch, and the powder-post beetle Dinoderus walks in through the cut ends to eat it. So the first defence is less starch: the right season, mature culms, and a week standing in shade.

Sources: Odisha Bamboo Development Agency (season, beetles, shade-curing) · Guadua Bamboo (age, flowering) · IS 9096 summary (puncturing diaphragms).

Make it last

Where will it live?
Soaking, step by step
  1. Fell, then shade-cure the culms upright for 7–10 days.
  2. Cut to length and puncture every diaphragm with a long steel rod, or drill each internode.
  3. Dissolve the boric acid and borax in water (warm water is faster) in a tank, pit or lined trench long enough for the culms.
  4. Sink the culms, weighted down; thicker walls need longer. ISO 22156 asks for about 4 kg of salts per m³ of bamboo indoors.
  5. Lift, drain on end, and dry slowly in shade to under 20 % moisture before building.

Ratio and strengths from Guadua Bamboo and the IS 9096 summary, which notes conflicting target moistures (25–30 % in IS 9096, ≤ 20 % in ISO). Awaiting builder review.

Finishes, paints and sealants

The last coat decides whether an earth wall can dry. Limewash, clay paint, casein, oils and waxes: what binds each one, how it sets, how open it stays to vapour, and how to mix it.

Six finishes

Can the wall still breathe?

What sd means, and sources

A finish's sd is the thickness of still air that would hold back water vapour as much as the finish does. EN ISO 7783 groups paints into four classes: under 0.14 m is highly breathable, over 1.5 m is low; heritage practice asks for under 0.05 m on solid walls. Grey points are published or makers' figures; clay-coloured points were measured in a lab to EN ISO 12572, and the two can disagree widely. In a 2024 study of Dublin walls, paint made little difference while the wall was moderately damp; near saturation, high-sd paints kept moisture in.

Sources: Walker et al. 2024 · Heritage Consulting · EN ISO 7783 classes · Keim · Earthborn. No measured sd was found for casein or linseed oil paint.

After a storm: how fast does the wall dry?

The drying model

Moisture leaves the wet layer by diffusion through two resistances in series, the wet earth (μ × 20 mm) and the finish (its sd); drying time is taken as proportional to their sum, with bare earth as 1. Earth μ measured on rammed earth: 16.6 by dry cup (Narloch et al. 2021) and 4 by wet cup (Stazi 2016, cited there). Relative times only; it ignores liquid flow, sun and wind. Illustrative Low.

What a lintel really carries

How an arch stands

The safe theorem, and the model behind the figure

Bricks and earth blocks have almost no strength in tension. An arch stands because the load finds a path of pure compression through it, the line of thrust. If any such line fits inside the ring, the arch can stand (the safe theorem); if it stays in the middle third, every joint stays closed. Where the line touches a face, the joint opens like a hinge; four hinges make a mechanism and it folds.

The figure loads the arch evenly across the span (its own weight at 18 kN/m³ plus the wall above), adds the point load if you set one, and searches for the thrust line that best fits the ring: the bending-moment diagram of a simple beam with the same loads, divided by the horizontal thrust H. It passes through the centre of each springing, which is slightly conservative. Thickness rules: Auroville Earth Institute (semicircular t = S/5, or S/10 with loaded haunches) and Boothby 2021 (S/t ≤ 40 r/S). Teaching model; awaiting builder review.

Structure and earthquakes

Earth walls are heavy and weak in tension. Pick your zone, shake two walls, and watch the five ways earth houses fail, each with its fix.

Your seismic zone

Z is the peak ground acceleration the code plans for, as a fraction of gravity. A revised code with a new Zone VI was withdrawn in March 2026, so the 2016 zones apply.

Shake two walls

How the model works, and what it leaves out

Each wall is a rigid block of earth with no tensile strength. The free wall rocks on its base corners (Housner's rocking equation, losing energy at each impact by the standard restitution); it starts to rock when ground acceleration exceeds g × t/h, and falls once it tips past its diagonal. The tied wall is held at the top, so it can only fail by folding at mid-height: by virtual work, the inertia W·a·h/4 must exceed the work against gravity W·t, so it needs 4 t/h × g, four times as much.

Left out: roof weight on the wall (it helps a tied wall and hurts a free one), cracking and damage, corners letting go, and the stronger shaking higher up a building. The record is synthetic, not a real earthquake. This is a teaching model, not a design check.

Sources: Lestuzzi 2023 (threshold t/h) · rocking of rigid blocks, arXiv 2106.11741 (Housner's equation, restitution) · zone factors, IS 1893 via InfraLens · withdrawal of IS 1893:2025. The 4 t/h result is derived here, not quoted.

Building physics

Heat, water vapour, humidity and air: what moves through an earth wall and a room, and why the natural materials behave so differently. Drag the round handles.

Heat and vapour through a wall

How the wall is worked out, and the values

Heat: the layers add up as resistances, R = Rsi + Σ d/λ + Rse, with U = 1/R and the surface resistances of the Indian residential code (0.13 and 0.04 m²K/W). Vapour: the Glaser method of EN ISO 13788. Each layer resists vapour like a thickness of still air, sd = μ·d; the vapour line runs straight from inside to outside in sd and may not rise above saturation; where it is forced down onto the saturation line, water condenses at the rate set by the change of slope.

Values: earth λ 0.95 (Cuitiño-Rosales 2020) with μ 16.6 (Narloch 2021, dry cup); CSEB λ 1.20 (Eco-Niwas Samhita) with μ 24 (6 % cement, Narloch); lime plaster λ 0.7, μ 7 (WUFI data); clay-straw plaster λ 0.34, μ 11 (Liuzzi 2016); straw bale λ 0.052 (Passive House Institute) with μ 1.5 assumed Low; acrylic paint sd 0.53 m (Walker 2024, measured); plastic sheet sd 6.6 m (WUFI generic retarder). Steady state only: it ignores the wall's moisture storage, rain and sun, which matter a great deal for earth. Use it to see the direction of risk, not to certify a wall.

Walls that breathe humidity

What the moisture buffer value means, and sources

The moisture buffer value (MBV) is the water a surface takes up per m² for each 1 % rise in humidity, measured over 8 hours at 75 % and 16 at 33 % (the NORDTEST method). Litres = MBV × area × 42 % ÷ 1000. Classes: under 0.5 limited, 0.5–1 moderate, 1–2 good, above 2 excellent (Rode et al. 2005). Clay plaster 1.56–1.89 and with paper 2.34–2.73 (Nutt 2020); gypsum, cellular concrete and hemp (Khaled 2023); spruce, brick, concrete (Rode 2005). Finishes cut buffering: linseed oil 39–50 %, beeswax 29 %, limewash 7–13 % (Santos et al. 2024, see Plasters and binders). When temperature and humidity swing together, a clay base coat buffered only 0.34–0.57 (Cascione 2019): treat the litres as a lab comparison, not a room forecast.

Air that moves itself

Stack effect

Height pulls air

Warm air inside is lighter than the cooler air outside. Give it a way out high and a way in low and the house ventilates itself, with no wind and no fan. The flow grows with the square root of the height between the openings and of the temperature difference: four times the height gives twice the air.

Q = C·A·√(2 g h ΔT / T), with C = 0.65 (stack effect), two equal openings in series (A/√2), room air at 30 °C, wind ignored. Room of 48 m³.

How big a tank?

How the tank is worked out, and sources

A day-by-day water balance. Each day the roof adds its plan area × that month's rain, spread evenly over the month's days, × a runoff coefficient: 0.85 for clay tiles and 0.8 for metal, inside the ranges of the KUIDFC trainers' manual (0.8–0.9 and 0.7–0.9). The household then draws its water; whatever does not fit in the tank overflows. Three years are run so each year starts as the last one ended. "Never runs dry" is the largest running shortfall over the year (the sequent-peak method), rounded up to 0.5 kL.

Rain: Begumpet monthly normals, 1991–2020, about 828 mm a year (Climate-Zone, from 23 years of records) Medium. An average year only: dry years come, so leave a margin. For scale, rural India plans at least 55 litres a person a day for everything (Jal Jeevan Mission guidelines, para 6.1, as quoted by the CAG). First flush, screens, covers and the floating intake: Texas Manual on Rainwater Harvesting (2005), pp. 8–18. A 150 mm pipe holds about 17.7 litres a metre.

The WEDC guide sets distance by ground (Reed 2014, table 2): silt 10 m, fine silty sand 15, weathered basement not fractured 25, medium sand 50, gravel 500; in fractured rock no horizontal distance protects a water source. Sludge (table 3): 40 litres a person a year in water and 60 dry, with degradable cleansing, plus 50 % for the shallow pits of twin-pit toilets. Sludge here: 0.04 (IS 12314, dry) to 0.06 m³ (WEDC, twin pit) a person a year; a pit 1 m across holds about 0.8 m³ for each metre of depth. Greywater into a garden

Horizontal-flow bed

Roots and stones do the work

Water from the kitchen, bath and laundry carries soap, grease and food. Settled first, it creeps sideways through a lined bed of gravel planted with reeds, below the surface, and bacteria living on the stones and roots clean it on the way. Not for raw toilet water.

Sizes from the Eawag Compendium (Tilley et al. 2014): about 5–10 m² for each person, gravel 3–32 mm, 0.5–1 m deep, water held 5–15 cm below the top. It needs a lot of land and removes little of the nutrients.
